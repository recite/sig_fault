\begin{abstract}
\noindent Finding a methodological error can improve later research only if researchers respond to it. We examine citations after audits of statistical practice in neuroscience, animal experiments, and political science. Our neuroscience design compares flagged and unflagged papers published in the same journal and year, allowing each journal/publication-year group its own citation trajectory and accounting for stable differences between articles. Flagged papers' median annual citations rose from \MedianFlaggedBefore{} before the critique to \MedianFlaggedMin--\MedianFlaggedMax{} afterward. A separate citation-context sample recorded no acknowledgment of concerns in \Nonack{} of \Rated{} valid completed ratings. The within-journal/year estimate of the proportional citation change is \JcWosPercent\% (95\% interval [\JcWosLower, \JcWosUpper]\%); rebuilding the same comparison with OpenAlex gives \JcOaPercent\%. The estimate remains similar when we additionally compare studies of the same species and within/between-subject design. The additional audits also show continued citation. The findings document persistent attention to criticized research, usually without a recorded qualification in the neuroscience context sample, alongside an estimated relative citation penalty whose magnitude remains uncertain.
\end{abstract}
\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} methodological criticism; citation analysis; scientific self-correction; statistical errors; citation contexts

\section{Introduction}
Finding a consequential error in published research is only the beginning of correcting it. Subsequent researchers must learn about the problem and reconsider how they use the affected work. Otherwise, an unsupported inference can continue to motivate hypotheses, justify a method, or enter a synthesis of evidence. Whether public criticism changes later research therefore matters for the value of the effort spent identifying and explaining errors.

Studies of retracted research show why disclosure alone may be insufficient. Retracted articles continue to receive citations, and many citing passages do not mention the retraction \citep{hsiao2021}. Citation behavior can reflect how researchers search for relevant work: unfamiliarity with a field and reliance on prominent journals are associated with continued citation of retracted articles \citep{woo2024}. Criticism may also remain outside the citing literature. Articles discussed critically on post-publication review platforms do not necessarily attract corresponding negative citations in research papers \citep{bordignon2020}. These findings make the transmission of corrective information an empirical question, even when the original article carries a formal warning.

Methodological criticism often supplies a less conspicuous warning. An audit can show that a statistical argument is invalid or that a result depends on a questionable analytical choice while the original article remains available in its published form. Researchers may have to connect a critique published elsewhere to the specific result they wish to cite. They may also reasonably continue to use portions of a paper that the criticism does not affect. The relevant questions are whether the affected papers lose citations relative to comparable papers and whether subsequent citations acknowledge the concern.

We examine those questions using four methodological audits. They concern an invalid comparison of statistical significance in neuroscience, pseudoreplication in animal experiments, weak instruments and sensitivity of inference in political science, and extrapolation in political-science interaction models. Each supplies paper-level assessments and a comparison group drawn from the same audit. These assessments range from identifiable errors in statistical reasoning to diagnostic concerns about an estimate. We preserve that distinction: a flagged diagnostic need not establish that the substantive finding is false, and an unflagged paper is not necessarily free of other problems.

Our outcome is citation to the original paper. The neuroscience design compares flagged and unflagged papers published in the same journal and year. These papers passed the same journals' editorial filters, come from the same assessment exercise, and have similar citation ages. Article fixed effects account for permanent differences in citation levels, while separate calendar-year effects for each journal/publication-year group account for different citation trajectories across those groups. We supplement this comparison with estimates from the other audits and an inverse-variance-weighted synthesis. We also examine citation levels and a historical sample of passages citing the neuroscience papers. Citation levels describe continued attention; comparative changes estimate how that attention differs from the trajectory of other assessed papers. A paper can remain widely cited and still receive fewer citations than it would have without criticism.

Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows the persistence of citation to the neuroscience papers in both medians and means. The within-journal/year comparison estimates a \JcWosPercent\% change in relative citation growth (95\% interval [\JcWosLower, \JcWosUpper]\%). The point estimate is similar when citation histories are rebuilt from OpenAlex and when the sample is restricted to papers with a full calendar year after publication before the critique. The design therefore compares citation growth among papers of similar age in the same journals, rather than attributing ordinary differences between journals or publication cohorts to the critique.

Failed replications raise a related but separate question. Prior work has examined citations after unsuccessful attempts to reproduce findings, including the Reproducibility Project: Psychology \citep{osc2015,serragarcia2021,schafmeister2021,vonhippel2022,clark2023}. Our analysis concerns published methodological criticisms of the original research. An unsuccessful replication does not by itself identify an error in the original analysis, so replication outcomes are not pooled with these audits.

\section{Data and research design}
\subsection{Audits, assessments, and publicity}
We assemble audits for which the original papers, methodological assessments, and publication histories can be recovered. This is a selected collection of audits, not a probability sample of scientific errors or a systematic review of every published critique. Within each audit, we preserve the source roster and classifications, document missing or unclear assessments, and distinguish the papers available for description from those contributing to estimation. Source files, identity decisions, publicity dates and numbered acquisition and analysis scripts accompany each study in the repository.

The neuroscience audit reviewed behavioral, systems, and cognitive neuroscience papers published in 2009--2010 in \textit{Nature}, \textit{Science}, \textit{Nature Neuroscience}, \textit{Neuron}, and sampled issues of the \textit{Journal of Neuroscience} \citep{nieuwenhuis2011}. It identified \Classified{} papers in which a comparison of effects was relevant and classified \ClassifiedFlagged{} as making the mistake. The mistake is to interpret statistical significance in one group and nonsignificance in another as evidence that the groups differ. Establishing that difference requires testing the contrast itself \citep{gelman2006}. The audit's authors supplied us with the paper-level classifications. Although their article did not list the affected papers, a reader who understood the critique could recognize the mistake in an original article. We use the August 2011 publication as the publicity episode.

The animal-study audit assessed \LazicPapers{} experiments in which interventions were applied to parents and outcomes measured in offspring \citep{lazic2018}. It classified \LazicAssessedFlagged{} as using pseudoreplication, \LazicAssessedComparison{} as correctly analyzed, and \LazicUnclear{} as too unclear to judge. Pseudoreplication treats dependent observations, such as offspring from the same treated litter, as independent experimental replications. The paper-level dataset and preprint became public in September 2017, before journal publication in April 2018. We exclude unclear assessments and one flagged paper criticized for the same problem in 2013. The descriptive comparison retains \LazicFlagged{} flagged and \LazicComparison{} comparison papers.

The instrumental-variable audit reanalyzed designs in \LalPapers{} political-science papers \citep{lal2024}. Our primary diagnostic flags a paper if any assessed design has an effective first-stage $F$ below 10. This is a screening threshold, not a universal definition of weak instruments. An alternative flags estimates significant under the archived analytic test but not under an Anderson--Rubin or applicable $tF$ procedure. That comparison is restricted to papers with at least one analytically significant estimate. These definitions capture different methodological concerns and identify overlapping groups. The audit appeared online in May 2024; an earlier manuscript is dated July 2021. We therefore study the additional publicity surrounding formal publication.

The interaction-model audit flags \HmxFlaggedPapers{} papers for severe extrapolation and leaves \HmxComparisonPapers{} unflagged on that diagnostic \citep{hainmueller2019}. The concern is that estimated conditional effects rely on combinations of predictors with little supporting data. Comparison papers may have other problems identified by the audit. Online journal publication occurred in December 2018, following earlier draft circulation and a July 2018 replication archive. Here too, formal publication is an episode of additional publicity, not an established first disclosure.

\subsection{Citation histories and citation contexts}
We count distinct citing documents per original paper and year, retaining citation-free years when the history has been collected. Missing histories remain missing. Historical Web of Science exports cover \Covered{} of the \Classified{} neuroscience papers. Two covered histories contain the intended original itself and records predating its publication, indicating invalid searches. Excluding both leaves \Flagged{} flagged and \Comparison{} comparison papers. The incomplete 2016 export is excluded from annual citation comparisons. Appendix~\ref{app:data} documents the exclusions and record-level checks.

OpenAlex supplies the instrumental-variable and interaction-audit histories; their primary counts include articles and reviews. OpenCitations supplies the animal-study histories, including all indexed document types. The databases and available document-type filters differ, so the synthesis combines comparisons within each audit rather than treating their citation levels as directly interchangeable. We also reconstruct the neuroscience histories in OpenAlex and OpenCitations to compare estimates on identical original papers and years (Appendix~\ref{app:extensions}).

The historical citation-context sample concerns citations to flagged neuroscience papers after the critique. It contains \Rated{} valid completed ratings. The protocol asks whether the citing text notes a concern about the cited paper; it does not require the concern to be the specific statistical error. Known false links, unavailable articles and unfinished ratings remain separately identified. The sample has no pre-critique coding, so it describes recorded acknowledgment after publicity rather than a change in acknowledgment. We do not infer the citing authors' awareness from these codes.

\subsection{Comparisons and estimand}
The target is the change in citations caused by publicizing a methodological problem. We estimate proportional citation changes for flagged papers relative to comparison papers, conditioning on the comparison groups specified below. A negative coefficient means that flagged papers' citations grew less, or fell more, proportionally. It does not require their citation counts to fall in absolute terms.

For each audit, we estimate a Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood model with article and calendar-year fixed effects:
\[
 E[C_{it}\mid i,t,F_i]=\exp\{\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta F_i P_t\},
\]
where $C_{it}$ is the citation count, $F_i$ denotes a flagged paper, and $P_t$ denotes the post-publicity period. Article effects absorb stable differences in citation levels; year effects absorb common calendar-year changes. We report $100(e^{\widehat\beta}-1)$ with article-clustered uncertainty. This approach retains observed zeros and does not require citation counts to have equal conditional mean and variance \citep{santos2006}. Papers with zero citations in every included year do not identify the coefficient and are recorded separately.

For the neuroscience comparison, we replace $\lambda_t$ with $\lambda_{s(i)t}$, where $s(i)$ is the article's journal and publication year. Each of the ten journal/publication-year groups contains both flagged and unflagged papers; all \Analyzed{} originals therefore have comparison support. The design allows, for example, papers published in \textit{Neuron} in 2009 to age differently from papers published in \textit{Science} in 2010. It identifies the coefficient from differential changes within these groups. We use 2010 as the baseline and 2012--2015 as the follow-up, excluding the 2011 transition year. The identifying assumption is that, absent the critique, flagged and unflagged papers within a group would have followed comparable proportional citation paths. We assess comparability using the original audit's species and study-design classifications and pre-critique citations, and estimate additional comparisons within those study types. An additive fixed-effects model estimates the corresponding difference in citation-count changes. Both models impose a common within-group contrast; they are not equal-weight averages of heterogeneous article-level effects.

The synthesis applies the common article-and-year specification to each audit and compares the calendar year before the selected publicity year with the calendar year after it, excluding the publicity year. The windows are 2010--2012 for neuroscience, 2016--2018 for animal studies, 2023--2025 for instrumental variables, and 2017--2019 for interaction models. We combine one log contrast per audit using inverse estimated sampling-variance weights. A paper shared by the two political-science audits is omitted from the interaction component to avoid counting the same original twice. The main synthesis contains \AuditPapers{} contributing papers. It summarizes these audits with their chosen definitions and windows; it is not a universal effect of discovering an error.

A causal interpretation requires the flagged and comparison groups to have followed comparable proportional citation trajectories without the publicity episode. Membership in an audit helps align the research area and assessment process, but error status was not randomized. Topics, prominence, citation age and prior criticism may differ across groups. Publicity can also affect comparison papers, so the contrast measures the differential response to being flagged rather than the audit's total effect on scientific activity. Earlier circulation limits interpretation as an effect of first disclosure. We examine longer windows, earlier trends, journal and publication-cohort adjustments, alternative diagnostics, and paper-level influence in the appendices.

\section{Continued citation and acknowledgment}
\subsection{Neuroscience citation trajectories}
Continued citation extends beyond a few prominent papers. Flagged papers' median annual citations rose from \MedianFlaggedBefore{} in 2010 to between \MedianFlaggedMin{} and \MedianFlaggedMax{} in each year from 2012 through 2015 (Figure~\ref{fig:paths}). Comparison papers' medians rose from \MedianComparisonBefore{} to \MedianComparisonMin--\MedianComparisonMax. Mean citations also rose: from \FlaggedBefore{} to a post-period annual average of \FlaggedAfter{} for flagged papers, and from \ComparisonBefore{} to \ComparisonAfter{} for comparison papers. For \IncreasedPercent\% of flagged papers, the post-period annual average exceeded the baseline.

OpenAlex gives \JcOaPercent\% [\JcOaLower, \JcOaUpper]\% on the same papers and years. The estimates suggest slower proportional growth for flagged papers, even as their citation counts rose. The interval allows both a larger penalty and an increase, so the design does not tightly determine the magnitude.

An additive comparison that gives each flagged paper equal weight estimates \DesignWosFullEqual{} additional citations per paper per year (95\% interval [\DesignWosFullEqualLower, \DesignWosFullEqualUpper]). It compares each flagged paper's change with the average change of comparison papers in its journal/publication-year group. Flagged papers began with more citations, so they can gain more citations while growing less proportionally. Figure~\ref{fig:matchedpaths} in the appendix retains the median-and-mean presentation while standardizing both groups to the flagged papers' journal/publication-year composition. Appendix~\ref{app:journalcohort} reports support, timing, source comparisons and the full-baseline-cohort sensitivity. The original specifications remain in Appendix~\ref{app:inference}.

\subsection{Acknowledgment in citing papers}
Of \Rated{} valid completed ratings, \Nonack{} recorded no acknowledgment of concerns and \Ack{} recorded acknowledgment. Qualification was therefore rare in the observed post-publicity sample. The result concerns the neuroscience papers and cannot establish the frequency of qualification in the other audits.

A citation without criticism need not endorse the disputed inference. It may use another finding, a method, or background information. The codes also cannot distinguish failure to encounter the critique from a decision that the criticism does not affect the particular use. What they establish is that readers of these citing passages were seldom alerted to a concern about the original paper. Appendix~\ref{app:data} reports the coding protocol, missing ratings and uncertainty.

\section{Citation changes across methodological audits}
\subsection{Citation levels and sensitivity within audits}
The instrumental-variable papers continued to receive citations after formal publication of the critique. Under the two main diagnostics, flagged papers' mean annual citations rose from \LalWeakBefore{} to \LalWeakAfter{} and from \LalSensitiveBefore{} to \LalSensitiveAfter{} between 2023 and 2025; their medians changed little. Comparisons with other assessed papers are imprecise and change substantially when measured from an earlier year. The differing signs under alternative diagnostics do not establish distinct behavioral responses to different statistical problems (Appendix~\ref{app:extensions}).

In the animal-study audit, citation levels changed little on average. From 2016 to 2019, flagged papers' mean annual citations changed from \LazicFlaggedBefore{} to \LazicFlaggedAfter{}, and comparison papers' from \LazicComparisonBefore{} to \LazicComparisonAfter{}. Their respective medians were \LazicFlaggedMedianBefore{} and \LazicFlaggedMedianAfter{}, versus \LazicComparisonMedianBefore{} and \LazicComparisonMedianAfter{}. The difference in mean changes is \LazicAbsolute{} citations per paper (95\% interval [\LazicAbsoluteLower, \LazicAbsoluteUpper]). Appendix~\ref{app:lazic} reports longer trajectories and alternative comparisons. The synthesis uses the adjacent-year 2016--2018 contrast defined above.

The interaction-model comparison is sensitive to a highly cited comparison paper. Mean citations rose from \HmxFlaggedMeanBefore{} to \HmxFlaggedMeanAfter{} among flagged papers and from \HmxComparisonMeanBefore{} to \HmxComparisonMeanAfter{} among comparison papers. The medians moved differently: \HmxFlaggedMedianBefore{} to \HmxFlaggedMedianAfter{} and \HmxComparisonMedianBefore{} to \HmxComparisonMedianAfter{}, respectively. The proportional contrast is \HmxPercent\% (95\% interval [\HmxLower, \HmxUpper]\%); omitting the influential comparison paper changes it to \HmxLooMax\%. Together with differences in earlier citation growth, this sensitivity limits interpreting the average contrast as a clear publication-related break (Appendix~\ref{app:hmx}).

The interval remains broad because article-level citation changes vary substantially and some comparisons contain few flagged or comparison papers. Highly cited papers can account for much of that variation. The synthesis uses only one pre-publicity year and one post-publicity year per audit, so it also retains annual fluctuations that longer histories could help distinguish from a sustained change. Its interval uses a normal critical value; the breadth does not arise from using a small-number-of-audits critical value. The repository provides an independent reconstruction of each standard error from paired citation counts.

The comparison assumptions pose a separate problem. In the interaction audit, slower relative growth precedes formal publication, and one comparison paper changes the sign of the post-publication estimate when omitted. In the instrumental-variable audit, changing the baseline year changes the estimates substantially. Both critiques circulated before journal publication. These findings weaken the interpretation of the pooled contrast as the effect of publicity. The reported interval does not account for bias from a wrong counterfactual or an incorrectly dated exposure. Appendix~\ref{app:synthesis} specifies the weighting and reports random-effects uncertainty.

The original article-and-year neuroscience comparison also gives similar results across citation sources. On the same original papers and years, the relative-growth estimate is \NwOaWosPercent\% in Web of Science and \NwOaPercent\% using OpenAlex articles and reviews. A separate full-cohort OpenCitations comparison is also similar. This agreement concerns the estimated contrast, not completeness of the databases: the indexes differ in links and publication dates. Appendix~\ref{app:extensions} documents the paired comparisons.

\section{Discussion}
The affected papers continued to receive citations after public methodological criticism. The neuroscience evidence shows continued citation in medians as well as means, accompanied by little recorded acknowledgment in the citation-context sample. The additional audits show continuing attention to papers flagged for other methodological problems. These observations address the persistence of the original research after criticism.

The comparative estimates address a different part of scientific correction: citations that criticism may have prevented. Their main precision-weighted point estimate indicates slower relative growth for flagged papers, but the magnitude changes with the diagnostic and comparison. Papers can remain influential while losing some of the citations they otherwise would have received. Our findings therefore do not support treating continued citation as proof that criticism has no effect, or treating a relative reduction as proof that the criticized inference has stopped circulating.

The distinction matters for how scientific criticism is communicated. A methodological article can teach readers to recognize an invalid inference, even without naming every affected paper. Making that connection still takes attention and judgment. Prominent citation histories do not show whether researchers have made it. The rarity of recorded qualifications shows that a concern is often absent from the citing text, while the citation counts reveal how much attention the original paper continues to receive. Neither measure alone establishes continued reliance on the particular disputed claim.

These audits also differ in what a reasonable response would entail. The neuroscience comparison of significance is an invalid argument for a difference between effects. Pseudoreplication misstates the amount of independent information in an experiment. Weak-instrument and extrapolation diagnostics instead question the reliability of particular estimates under particular assumptions. Such criticism can require a revised interpretation without making every use of the article inappropriate. We do not equate all flagged papers with false findings.

The within-journal/year design addresses differences in journals and citation age; its identifying assumption concerns remaining differences in growth within those groups. The additional audits have less uniform comparisons: some critiques circulated before journal publication, and earlier citation changes differ across diagnostic groups. Comparison papers may also respond to the critique. Our source cohorts are selected rather than representative of all methodological errors. The results describe the differential citation response around documented publicity episodes under explicit comparison assumptions. Establishing how criticism changes use of an erroneous claim would additionally require tracing that claim through later papers and observing whether the later argument actually depends on it.

\section{Within-journal and publication-year comparisons}\label{app:journalcohort}
The ten comparison groups are the five audited journals crossed with the two original-publication years. All contain both flagged and unflagged papers; the smallest contains three flagged papers and one comparison paper. Article effects absorb fixed citation levels, and group-by-calendar-year effects allow each group its own path. The main estimation retains all \Analyzed{} papers. Standard errors cluster by article with the same finite-sample convention as the other models.

The repository's \href{https://github.com/recite/sig_fault/tree/main/data/nieuwenhuis/design}{journal/year analysis} records the group counts, article weights, model samples and annual estimates. It reports both citation sources, both outcome scales, the full sample and the 2009 publication cohort, and the longer and first-year follow-ups. The 2009 cohort has a full calendar-year baseline after original publication. A separate 2009--2010 contrast uses a partial publication-year baseline and is reported as a timing diagnostic, not a clean test of prior parallel trends. Annual intervals are pointwise.

The study-characteristic balance check finds that \BalanceHumanFlagged\% of flagged papers study humans, compared with \BalanceHumanComparison\% of comparison papers after journal/year standardization. We therefore also compare papers within human/animal and within/between-subject groups, retaining \DesignMatchedFlagged{} flagged and \DesignMatchedComparison{} comparison papers. A stricter species-and-design comparison retains \DesignSpeciesFlagged{} and \DesignSpeciesComparison{}, respectively. Each sensitivity first repeats the journal/year model on the retained sample, so the additional adjustment is not confused with a change in population. Table~\ref{tab:designchecks} shows similar proportional estimates. Country labels and baseline citation levels are not exactly balanced; their full diagnostics accompany the data. These checks support the comparison against specific observed differences, rather than establishing random assignment.

The fixed-effects models and the equal-flagged-paper contrast have different weights. With this common-date, balanced design, the linear coefficient weights each group's additive contrast in proportion to $n_{1s}n_{0s}/(n_{1s}+n_{0s})$. Those weights are nonnegative. The equal-flagged contrast instead uses $n_{1s}/\sum_s n_{1s}$, allowing heterogeneous group effects while giving each flagged paper equal weight. Its interval uses HC3 uncertainty from the equivalent weighted article-change regression, with residual degrees of freedom. Some finer groups contain a single comparison paper, so this interval is a model-based approximation rather than exact within-group randomization inference. The repository records outcome weights, leverage and variance contributions. It also records Poisson information shares, which describe curvature of the fitted objective rather than robust sampling variance or an exact weighting of heterogeneous percentage effects.

\section{Models, sensitivity, and uncertainty}\label{app:inference}
\subsection{Proportional changes}
The original article-and-year specification, retained for comparison with the within-journal/year design, is
\[
 E[C_{it}\mid i,t,F_i]=\exp\{\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta F_i P_t\},
\]
where $F_i$ denotes a flagged paper and $P_t$ denotes 2012--2015. Article effects absorb stable differences in citation levels and year effects absorb common calendar-year changes. Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood retains zero counts and does not require equality of conditional mean and variance for the robust inference used here \citep{santos2006}. The reported percentage is $100(e^\beta-1)$, a relative post/pre citation change rather than a percentage change in the number of additional citations gained. In this balanced baseline specification, $e^{\widehat\beta}$ equals the ratio of the groups' post/pre mean citation ratios.

The covariance clusters score contributions by paper. We apply the $G/(G-1)$ and $(N-1)/(N-K)$ corrections, counting fixed effects not nested within article clusters in $K$, and use $t_{G-1}$ critical values. Table~\ref{tab:proportional} reports both two-sided 95\% intervals and one-sided 95\% lower bounds, transformed from the log scale. These are conditional model-based comparisons, not randomized-treatment intervals. No papers are excluded from this neuroscience follow-up fit for zero outcomes.

Comparing the two groups' mean changes gives \MainEstimate{} (95\% Welch interval \MainInterval), allowing unequal variances of paper-level changes. OLS with article and year fixed effects on the same balanced panel reproduces this point estimate exactly; it is another implementation of the same comparison, not independent corroboration. The generated results also report its article-clustered interval. Journal and publication-year adjustment in the paper-level change regression uses HC3 uncertainty.

\section{Synthesis across methodological audits}\label{app:synthesis}
For each audit, we estimate the log of the flagged group's post/pre mean citation ratio divided by the comparison group's ratio. We weight these contrasts by inverse estimated sampling variance: $w_j=s_j^{-2}/\sum_k s_k^{-2}$ and $\bar\beta=\sum_j w_j\widehat\beta_j$. The reported quantity is $100(e^{\bar\beta}-1)$, with standard error $1/\sqrt{\sum_j s_j^{-2}}$ on the log scale. Normal intervals condition on the included audits and estimated component variances. They summarize a precision-weighted average and assume independence across audit estimates. Weighting, timing, and selection of audits are retrospective choices.

The windows are 2010 versus 2012 for neuroscience, 2023 versus 2025 for instrumental variables, 2016 versus 2018 for animal studies, and 2017 versus 2019 for the interaction audit. These are post/pre ratios, not annualized growth rates. For animal studies, 2018 follows the first public release but coincides with journal publication. Earlier circulation precedes formal publication of the instrumental-variable and interaction audits. The longer neuroscience and animal-study comparisons in the main text address persistence over their respective follow-up periods and do not replace these adjacent-year components.

Vernby (2013) appears in both the interaction and instrumental-variable audits. We omit it from the interaction component, while retaining it in the standalone interaction analysis. Papers with no citations in either comparison year contribute no information to the Poisson coefficient; they remain in descriptive summaries. The main synthesis contains \AuditPapers{} contributing original papers. No known original DOI overlaps the resulting components. Distinct originals may still share citing papers or correlated field shocks, which the component-wise sampling variances do not model.

The alternative instrumental-variable diagnostic replaces the instrument-strength component with the inferential-sensitivity component, including its restricted comparison population. It does not add an independent study. Citation-source substitutions and leave-one-audit-out estimates similarly change a comparison rather than increase the amount of independent evidence. The random-effects sensitivity uses restricted maximum likelihood and modified Knapp--Hartung intervals with three degrees of freedom. Its adjustment cannot shrink uncertainty below the unadjusted value. It estimates a modeled mean across audit effects; it is not a prediction for an unobserved critique. The result is \AuditReWeakPercent\% (95\% interval [\AuditReWeakLower, \AuditReWeakUpper]\%).

The Institute for Replication pilot contains only \IfrCases{} selected disclosures and remains excluded from pooling. Other source inventories do not enter until their identities, assessment definitions, publicity timing and citation comparisons have been established. Failed-replication citation analyses concern a different assessment and are not components of this synthesis.

\section{Matched cases with individually publicized errors}\label{app:i4r}

The I4R registry separates catalog listings, article-specific assessments and verified error disclosures.
