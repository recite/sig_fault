\begin{abstract}
\noindent Papers can remain widely cited even when adverse assessments reduce their citation growth. We examine four methodological audits and a psychology replication project. In the neuroscience audit, flagged papers' median annual citations rose from \MedianFlaggedBefore{} before the critique to \MedianFlaggedMin--\MedianFlaggedMax{} afterward. A separate citation-context sample recorded no acknowledgment of concerns in \Nonack{} of \Rated{} valid completed ratings. The psychology project permits a comparison of \RppFailed{} papers with unsuccessful replications and \RppSuccessful{} with successful replications. Following its 2015 announcement, unsuccessful replications had a \RppReduction\% smaller post/pre citation ratio (95\% CI \mbox{[\RppLower, \RppUpper]\%}), standardizing journal composition. Across five studies, inverse-variance weighting gives a relative citation-growth contrast of \AssessmentWeakPercent\% [\AssessmentWeakLower, \AssessmentWeakUpper]\%; an alternative instrumental-variable diagnostic gives \AssessmentSensitivePercent\% [\AssessmentSensitiveLower, \AssessmentSensitiveUpper]\%. These estimates summarize the included studies and compare adverse with comparison assessments; they do not identify a universal response to errors or isolate the effect of first disclosure. The evidence supports continued citation alongside a modest relative citation penalty, whose magnitude depends on the assessment definition and comparison.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Publicizing a statistical error gives researchers a reason to reconsider their reliance on affected findings. A substantial response could take two forms: fewer citations to papers containing the error, or more citations that acknowledge the problem. We examine both after a prominent methodological critique in neuroscience, then compare citation changes following two further methodological audits and a psychology replication project.

The mistake is to treat a statistically significant effect in one group and a nonsignificant effect in another as evidence that the groups differ. That conclusion requires a test of the difference itself. Significance in one group can reflect greater precision rather than a larger effect \citep{gelman2006}. The missing test leaves the claimed contrast unsupported by that argument; it does not establish that every conclusion in the paper is false.

\citet{nieuwenhuis2011} identified \Classified{} papers in which a comparison of effects was relevant and classified \ClassifiedFlagged{} as making the mistake. They supplied us with the paper-level classifications. Researchers who understand the critique can recognize the mistake in an original paper and reconsider its use. The critique therefore need not name each affected paper to influence citations.

The affected papers remained widely cited, and recorded qualifications were rare. Median as well as mean citations increased after the critique; \IncreasedFlagged{} of \Flagged{} flagged papers received more citations per year in 2012--2015 than in 2010. Almost all valid completed ratings in our citation-context sample recorded no concern. The comparison with unflagged papers is less conclusive about how much publicity changed citation growth. We distinguish that causal question from the observed persistence of largely unqualified citation.

The expanded analysis asks whether papers receiving adverse assessments experience slower citation growth than comparison papers assessed by the same projects. An unsuccessful replication does not establish a statistical error, and an unflagged paper may itself benefit from favorable publicity. We preserve those distinctions while combining the studies' relative-growth contrasts. The pooled estimate describes these assembled studies, rather than a representative sample of scientific criticism.

\section{Data and comparison}
The source review covers behavioral, systems, and cognitive neuroscience papers published in 2009--2010 in \textit{Nature}, \textit{Science}, \textit{Nature Neuroscience}, \textit{Neuron}, and sampled issues of the \textit{Journal of Neuroscience}. The classification concerns the particular statistical inference reviewed, not the validity of every result in a paper.

The archived citation exports cover \Covered{} of the \Classified{} classified papers. Two further histories contain the intended cited paper itself and records predating it, indicating invalid citation searches. Excluding these histories leaves \Flagged{} flagged and \Comparison{} comparison papers. We count distinct citing documents per paper and year, retain citation-free years within covered histories, and exclude the incomplete 2016 export. Appendix~\ref{app:data} documents coverage, duplicates, and linkage checks.

The critique appeared online on August 26, 2011. We use 2010 as the baseline and 2012--2015 as the post-publicity period, omitting the transition year 2011. The main regression is Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood with article and calendar-year fixed effects. It compares proportional citation changes in flagged and comparison papers; uncertainty is clustered by article. A relative post/pre citation ratio below one indicates slower proportional growth for flagged papers. Appendix~\ref{app:inference} gives the specification and alternative comparisons.

Interpreting that contrast as an effect of publicity requires comparable proportional citation trajectories without the critique. Error status was not randomized, and papers may differ in topic, prominence, and ordinary citation growth. A general warning could also affect both groups; the comparison concerns their differential response, not the critique's total influence on science.

\section{Continued citation and qualification}
\subsection{Citation trajectories}
Continued citation extends beyond a few prominent papers. Flagged papers' median annual citations rose from \MedianFlaggedBefore{} in 2010 to between \MedianFlaggedMin{} and \MedianFlaggedMax{} in each year from 2012 through 2015 (Figure~\ref{fig:paths}). Comparison papers' medians rose from \MedianComparisonBefore{} to \MedianComparisonMin--\MedianComparisonMax. Mean citations also rose: from \FlaggedBefore{} to a post-period annual average of \FlaggedAfter{} for flagged papers, and from \ComparisonBefore{} to \ComparisonAfter{} for comparison papers. For \IncreasedPercent\% of flagged papers, the post-period annual average exceeded the baseline.

The main regression estimates a \ProportionalReduction\% smaller post/pre citation ratio for flagged papers (95\% interval [\ProportionalLower, \ProportionalUpper]\%). Thus, widespread continued citation coexists with considerable uncertainty about a relative citation penalty. The medians establish that continued citation was typical, while the regression addresses how growth compares with other papers. Neither an observed increase nor an imprecise comparison establishes that publicity had no effect.

Allowing citation trajectories to differ by journal and publication cohort produces estimates from \AdjustedPercentMin\% to \AdjustedPercentMax\%. Absolute citation gains instead favor flagged papers by \MainEstimate{} citations per paper per year (95\% interval \MainInterval): flagged papers started from a higher citation level, so absolute and proportional changes answer different questions. The appendix reports the full comparisons, later response windows, and bounds on the magnitude of a relative decline.

\subsection{Qualifications in citing papers}
The archived citation-context sample contains \CodingN{} relationships selected after 2011, including \PartialYear{} from the incomplete 2016 export. All concern citations to flagged papers. Of \Rated{} valid completed ratings, \Nonack{} recorded no acknowledgment of concerns and \Ack{} recorded acknowledgment. Unqualified citation was therefore common in the observed sample. The protocol records any concern about the cited paper, not only the specific interaction error.

A citation without criticism need not endorse the faulty inference. It may use another finding, a method, or background information. Nor do the codes reveal whether the authors recognized the mistake. Because the coding sample covers only the post-publicity period, it establishes the rarity of recorded qualifications after publicity, not how their frequency changed. Appendix~\ref{app:data} reports missing ratings and uncertainty.

\section{Additional evidence from an instrumental-variables audit}
A second methodological critique permits a comparison across fields and statistical problems. \citet{lal2024} reanalyze instrumental-variable designs in \LalPapers{} political-science papers. We recover citation histories for that entire assessed-paper cohort. These classifications concern instrument strength and sensitivity of inference; they do not establish that the original substantive conclusions are false. The comparison therefore extends the citation design to adverse diagnostics while keeping that distinction from demonstrated errors explicit.

We examine two main classifications. The first flags an effective first-stage $F$ below 10, a screening threshold rather than a universal criterion for weak instruments. The second flags a reported estimate that is significant at $p<.05$ under the archived analytic test but not under the archived Anderson--Rubin or applicable $tF$ procedure. This second comparison is restricted to papers with at least one analytically significant estimate. A paper satisfies a diagnostic if any of its assessed designs does. Table~\ref{tab:lal} also reports their union and the narrower Anderson--Rubin comparison; these overlapping definitions are alternative analyses of one audit.

The audit appeared online in May 2024, but an earlier manuscript is dated July 2021. We compare 2023 with 2025 and omit 2024. This contrast concerns citation changes around formal publication, not the effect of first exposure to previously unknown criticism. We count OpenAlex articles and reviews, deduplicate within original-paper citation histories, retain observed zero years, and fit the same article/year fixed-effects Poisson specification with article-clustered uncertainty. All included papers were published before the baseline year. Appendix~\ref{app:extensions} describes counting and diagnostic checks.

Papers flagged by the audit continued to receive citations. The historical neuroscience exports and the IV citation histories also come from different databases. Full-cohort comparisons with OpenAlex and OpenCitations yield similar neuroscience relative-growth point estimates (Appendix~\ref{app:extensions}).

\section{An audit of pseudoreplication in animal studies}
\citet{lazic2018} examined \LazicPapers{} animal studies in which interventions were applied to parents and outcomes measured in offspring. They classified \LazicAssessedFlagged{} as using pseudoreplication, \LazicAssessedComparison{} as correctly analyzed, and \LazicUnclear{} as too unclear to judge. Pseudoreplication treats dependent observations, such as offspring from the same treated litter, as independent experimental replications. These are the auditors' assessments of the studied designs; the deposited data do not supply corrected effect sizes.

We recover the entire identified sample and complete OpenCitations histories for all \LazicPapers{} papers. The preprint and paper-level dataset became public in September 2017; journal publication followed in April 2018. Our primary comparison uses 2016 and 2019, excluding the unclear assessments and one flagged paper publicly criticized for the same problem in 2013. This leaves \LazicFlagged{} flagged and \LazicComparison{} comparison papers.

Mean annual citations changed from \LazicFlaggedBefore{} to \LazicFlaggedAfter{} among flagged papers and from \LazicComparisonBefore{} to \LazicComparisonAfter{} among comparison papers. The corresponding medians were \LazicFlaggedMedianBefore{} and \LazicFlaggedMedianAfter{}, versus \LazicComparisonMedianBefore{} and \LazicComparisonMedianAfter{}. The difference in mean changes is \LazicAbsolute{} citations per paper (95\% interval [\LazicAbsoluteLower, \LazicAbsoluteUpper]). Citation levels changed little on average in both groups; this comparison supplies no clear evidence of an additional decline among flagged papers. Appendix~\ref{app:lazic} reports trajectories, proportional changes and sensitivity checks. As in the other audits, attributing the difference to publicity requires comparable citation paths in its absence.

\section{Individually publicized errors}
The Institute for Replication extension currently supports matched comparisons for only \IfrCases{} affected papers. This is a pilot, too small and selected to establish how citations generally respond to publicized errors. The source inventory is much larger, but most records still lack a verified consequential error, an established first-disclosure date, sufficient citation history, or complete controls. Appendix~\ref{app:i4r} retains the case comparisons and synthetic-control checks.

\section{Psychology replications and subsequent citations}
The Reproducibility Project: Psychology completed 100 replications of studies published in three psychology journals in 2008 \citep{osc2015}. They concern \RppPapers{} distinct original papers: the source judgments classify \RppFailed{} as unsuccessful and \RppSuccessful{} as successful replications. Both repeated-paper pairs have unsuccessful judgments. We verify every original identity against publisher records, preserving the source judgments and documented bibliographic corrections. OpenAlex supplies complete citation histories for all originals through 2025.

The project announced its findings in August 2015. We compare mean annual citations during 2012--2014 with 2016--2018, excluding the announcement year. Earlier individual reports mean that this contrast concerns additional project publicity. Within each journal, we calculate the ratio of unsuccessful papers' post/pre mean citations to successful papers' ratio, then average the log ratios using the full cohort's journal shares. The bootstrap resamples original papers within journal and replication group. Appendix~\ref{app:rpp} documents the panel, timing evidence and external comparisons.

Unsuccessful replications had a \RppReduction\% smaller post/pre citation ratio (95\% CI \mbox{[\RppLower, \RppUpper]\%}). Their observed mean annual citations changed from \RppFailedMeanBefore{} to \RppFailedMeanAfter{} and their median from \RppFailedMedianBefore{} to \RppFailedMedianAfter{}. For successful replications, the corresponding means were \RppSuccessfulMeanBefore{} and \RppSuccessfulMeanAfter{}, and the medians \RppSuccessfulMedianBefore{} and \RppSuccessfulMedianAfter{}. Thus, papers with unsuccessful replications continued to receive substantial citation, but their growth lagged behind the successful group. The journal-standardized absolute contrast is \RppLevel{} annual citations per paper (95\% interval [\RppLevelLower, \RppLevelUpper]). The within-project comparison combines any penalty for an unsuccessful replication with any publicity benefit for a successful one.

\section{Interaction-model diagnostics and subsequent citations}
An audit of multiplicative interaction models flags \HmxFlaggedPapers{} papers for severe extrapolation and leaves \HmxComparisonPapers{} unflagged on that diagnostic \citep{hainmueller2019}. The concern is that estimated conditional effects rely on combinations of predictors with little supporting data. Comparison papers can have other problems identified by the audit. We compare 2017 with 2019, around its December 2018 online journal publication. Earlier draft circulation and a July 2018 replication archive make this a comparison around additional publicity.

Flagged papers had a \HmxReduction\% smaller post/pre citation ratio than comparison papers (95\% interval [\HmxLower, \HmxUpper]\%). Mean citations rose from \HmxFlaggedMeanBefore{} to \HmxFlaggedMeanAfter{} among flagged papers and from \HmxComparisonMeanBefore{} to \HmxComparisonMeanAfter{} among comparison papers. The medians moved differently: \HmxFlaggedMedianBefore{} to \HmxFlaggedMedianAfter{} and \HmxComparisonMedianBefore{} to \HmxComparisonMedianAfter{}, respectively. Omitting one highly cited comparison paper changes the proportional estimate to \HmxLooMax\%. This small audit therefore provides an imprecise comparison of mean citation growth, sensitive to the upper tail, rather than evidence of a clear publication-related break. Appendix~\ref{app:hmx} documents the diagnostics, timing and influence checks.

\section{Citation changes across five studies}
We combine one log relative-growth contrast from each methodological audit with the psychology replication contrast, weighting by inverse estimated sampling variance. The main specification uses the instrument-strength diagnostic in the political-science audit. Its five components contain \AssessmentPapers{} contributing original papers. The estimated weighted contrast is \AssessmentWeakPercent\% (95\% interval [\AssessmentWeakLower, \AssessmentWeakUpper]\%). Substituting the alternative IV diagnostic gives \AssessmentSensitivePercent\% [\AssessmentSensitiveLower, \AssessmentSensitiveUpper]\% (Table~\ref{tab:assessment}). Both point estimates indicate slower relative citation growth after adverse assessments; their sensitivity to timing and inference is reported in the appendix.

\section{Discussion}
The affected papers continued to circulate after their statistical mistake was publicized. This pattern appears in median as well as mean citations and is accompanied by very little recorded qualification in the context sample. The evidence is inconsistent with wholesale abandonment of these papers. The larger set of citation comparisons nevertheless suggests a modest relative penalty after adverse assessments. Continued citation and a penalty can coexist: an affected paper may receive many citations while receiving fewer than it otherwise would have.

Citation growth already differed before the critique. Among papers published in 2009, flagged papers' increase from 2009 to 2010 exceeded that of comparison papers by \PretrendEstimate{} citations (95\% interval \PretrendInterval). That short publication-year baseline cannot establish the later counterfactual trajectory. Awareness is also unobserved, and some later citations may concern findings unaffected by the mistake. These limits leave room for a citation penalty even as the affected papers remain widely cited.

The practical question is how methodological criticism reaches the point of use. Readers can recognize an error from the original analysis, but doing so requires connecting a general warning to a particular paper. Our results document continued citation with little recorded acknowledgment; they do not identify which step in that process accounts for it.

\section{Models, sensitivity, and uncertainty}\label{app:inference}
\subsection{Proportional changes}
For paper $i$ in year $t$, the main model is
\[
 E[C_{it}\mid i,t,F_i]=\exp\{\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta F_i P_t\},
\]
where $F_i$ denotes a flagged paper and $P_t$ denotes 2012--2015. Article effects absorb stable differences in citation levels and year effects absorb common calendar-year changes. Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood retains zero counts and does not require equality of conditional mean and variance for the robust inference used here \citep{santos2006}. The reported percentage is $100(e^\beta-1)$, a relative post/pre citation change rather than a percentage change in the number of additional citations gained. In this balanced baseline specification, $e^{\widehat\beta}$ equals the ratio of the groups' post/pre mean citation ratios.

The covariance clusters score contributions by paper. We apply the $G/(G-1)$ and $(N-1)/(N-K)$ corrections, counting fixed effects not nested within article clusters in $K$, and use $t_{G-1}$ critical values. Table~\ref{tab:proportional} reports both two-sided 95\% intervals and one-sided 95\% lower bounds, transformed from the log scale. These are conditional model-based comparisons, not randomized-treatment intervals. No papers are excluded from the main fit for zero outcomes.

\section{Additional-cohort construction and comparability}\label{app:extensions}
The IV replication archive contains multiple designs for some papers. We retain the design records but assign each paper one citation history and equal weight within its diagnostic group. The repository reproduces the point estimates for the \LalVerifiedDesigns{} designs selected in the separate citation-context pilot. For the Alt design, the instrument is an eight-level factor with seven excluded indicators. Recomputing the joint Anderson--Rubin test with that encoding corrects the archived calculation; its loss-of-significance classification remains the same. The single-instrument $tF$ calculation is inapplicable and does not enter that design's sensitivity classification. These checks do not constitute independent replication of every diagnostic in the full audit.

The relative citation-growth estimates are \LalWeakChange\% for the weak-instrument screen and \LalSensitiveChange\% for inferential sensitivity. On a fixed older cohort, moving the baseline from 2023 to 2022 changes the weak-instrument estimate from \LalWeakOlderBaseline\% to \LalWeakAlternate\%, and the sensitivity estimate from \LalSensitiveOlderBaseline\% to \LalSensitiveAlternate\%. Holding the cohort fixed shows that changing the starting year alone can substantially change the comparison.

Document types materially affect the IV comparison. The original broad outcome included preprints, proceedings and book chapters; some books contributed links from numerous chapters and front matter to the same original paper. After observing this problem and the alternative estimates, we selected articles and reviews as the primary outcome. For the weak-instrument screen, the broader count gives \LalWeakBroad\%, compared with \LalWeakChange\% for articles and reviews. This was an outcome-aware measurement revision. The repository retains the broader results and a partial sensitivity that collapses identified Oxford book DOI families; neither procedure resolves all cross-version duplication.

The source-comparison protocol preserves the original neuroscience analysis cohort and compares identical papers and years before considering a pooled result. Exact DOI matches identify shared citing relationships, with each database's publication year retained. Historical exports lack the detailed document-type field needed to impose an identical article/review filter. Missing histories stay missing; a relationship absent from one database is not automatically a false citation. Source discrepancies therefore measure differences between the recorded citation histories rather than bias relative to an assumed ground truth.

The complete paired comparison contains \NwPairedFlagged{} flagged and \NwPairedComparison{} comparison papers. Replacing the historical counts with OpenAlex articles and reviews gives a relative-growth contrast of \NwOaPercent\% (95\% interval \mbox{[\NwOaLower, \NwOaUpper]\%}), compared with \NwOaWosPercent\% in the historical exports. The broader OpenAlex document-type count gives a contrast of \NwOaBroadPercent\%. These are comparisons of recorded citation histories on fixed papers and years; neither database is assumed to be ground truth.

Among \NwYearMismatch{} shared citation relationships with conflicting years, OpenAlex dates \NwEarlier{} earlier. Publisher-deposited online and print dates explain \NwDateExplained{} differences in the reviewed links; the remaining dates have not all been independently reviewed. Moving shared eligible citations between years contributes \NwBaselineShift{} citations to the \NwBaselineGap{}-citation difference in the 2010 totals. Coverage and publication dating can therefore both change measured growth.

For the \NwValidationPapers{} paired papers checked against OpenCitations, that index also records \NwExtraPrimaryCorroborated{} of the \NwExtraPrimary{} OpenAlex-only links eligible for the primary article/review count. Across all document types in the comparison frame, it records \NwExtraCorroborated{} of \NwExtraAll{} OpenAlex-only links. \section{Pseudoreplication audit: construction and sensitivity}\label{app:lazic}
The source sampled eligible English-language prenatal-exposure experiments in multiparous mammals from 500 PubMed search results. Our population is the \LazicPapers{} deposited assessments, not animal research generally. Exact PMID matching identifies all \LazicPapers{} papers; \LazicDoiPapers{} also have a DOI. We retain the \LazicNoDoiPapers{} papers without DOI and query their citations by PMID. The first preprint appeared on September 2, 2017, and the identified dataset was released on September 6. The repository documents the release dates and source provenance.

OpenCitations supplies \LazicLinks{} relationships. Citing records sharing a DOI or OpenCitations identity count once per target; \LazicUndated{} relationships have no assignable year. Counts include all indexed document types, including distinct book chapters. A check of \LazicReviewedChapters{} chapter--target pairs in three prominent book families verifies separate bibliographies containing the target references. We retain those chapters. A completed API history permits observed zero years; acquisition failure would not. The repository records identities, source checksums, citation links and notice adjudications.

One flagged paper received a public methodological criticism on May 17, 2013, with a response disputing the criticism. Earlier errata for other papers include name and affiliation corrections; two notices remain incompletely reviewed. \section{Psychology replication analysis: construction and checks}\label{app:rpp}
The original source contains 100 completed study records from \RppPapers{} papers. We retain all completed records, including one without a reported replication $p$ value, and use the source's replication judgment. Two original papers each have two completed replication records; both judgments for each paper are unsuccessful. Three sourced identity decisions correct a title typo, malformed punctuation, and a journal/page locator that belonged to another paper. Original source files and the identity-decision ledger remain available in the repository.

The citation panel deduplicates citing works within original papers and checks the original work's presence in each citing record's reference list. The primary outcome includes OpenAlex articles and reviews; all document types are a sensitivity. Conflicting DOI records retain an explicit resolution ledger. Incomplete histories remain missing; zeros require completed collection. Project logs preserve earlier report candidates, but filenames and upload dates alone do not establish historical public access to their contents.

For journal $j$, let $\overline C_{gjt}$ denote mean annual citations in replication group $g$ and period $t$. The proportional contrast is
\[
 \widehat\beta_{\mathrm{RPP}}=\sum_j q_j\left[
 \log\frac{\overline C_{\mathrm{unsuccessful},j,\mathrm{post}}}{\overline C_{\mathrm{unsuccessful},j,\mathrm{pre}}}
 -\log\frac{\overline C_{\mathrm{successful},j,\mathrm{post}}}{\overline C_{\mathrm{successful},j,\mathrm{pre}}}\right],
\]
where $q_j$ is the journal's share of the original-paper cohort. The journal shares remain fixed in 9,999 bootstrap resamples within journal and replication group. We report a percentile interval for this study and use the standard deviation of the log-scale bootstrap draws in the meta-analysis. We do not infer that standard error from the asymmetric percentage interval. All draws are defined. The absolute-change comparison uses journal-specific differences in mean changes and Welch--Satterthwaite inference.

External comparisons use original research papers from the same journals and 2008 print-publication year. They exclude known replication-assessed originals, nonresearch items and currently retracted papers. Matching uses only 2010--2014 citations. Three-nearest-neighbor matches use standardized log counts; synthetic controls fit standardized raw counts with nonnegative weights summing to one. The synthetic-control predictions use the same raw-count outcome. Reused donors retain their original article identities. Nearest-neighbor intervals cluster by original article and matched set; synthetic comparisons report point estimates and pre-period fit.

\section{Interaction-model audit: sample, timing and influence}\label{app:hmx}
The published audit's Table A1 records 46 interactions in 22 original papers. We preserve all interactions and aggregate each diagnostic separately: any adverse result flags a paper; if none is adverse but a test is missing, the paper remains unknown. Severe-extrapolation labels are complete. Six of the eight comparison papers have another adverse diagnostic. Neither this support diagnostic nor a failure to reject equal low/high effects establishes that the original substantive conclusion is false.

Publisher-deposited references identify every original, with author, print year and journal checked against publisher metadata. The version-one replication archive, publicly released on July 28, 2018, contains the 22-paper roster and analyses. It does not establish that all final binary labels appeared in the same form then. An SSRN draft was first posted in February 2016, but the earliest roster remains unverified. The event is therefore the December 18, 2018 online publication. The authors' December 2019 appendix update and response to subsequent methodological criticism fall within the longer follow-up sensitivity.

Complete OpenAlex citation links supply the annual panel. The primary outcome counts distinct citing articles and reviews. One paper, Williams (2011), has zero citations in both 2017 and 2019; it stays in descriptive means and medians but contributes no information to the Poisson coefficient. The standalone fit includes \HmxContributing{} contributing papers; excluding Vernby (2013) for the meta-analysis leaves \HmxMetaContributing{}. These exclusions follow the sample and estimation rules written before citation acquisition.

Article and year fixed-effects Poisson estimation yields the same coefficient as the log ratio of the two groups' post/pre mean citations. Article-clustered intervals use a $t_{G-1}$ critical value, where $G$ is the number of contributing papers. The within-group paired article bootstrap uses 9,999 draws and gives [\HmxBootLower, \HmxBootUpper]\%. The absolute difference between changes is \HmxAbsolute{} citations per paper per year (Welch interval [\HmxAbsoluteLower, \HmxAbsoluteUpper]). Leave-one-paper-out proportional estimates range from \HmxLooMin\% to \HmxLooMax\%; the positive endpoint omits Huddy, Mason and Aar\o e (2015). The negative earlier-trend contrast also cautions against interpreting the post-publication comparison as a newly emerging difference.

The repository reports 2019--2021 follow-up, all citing document types, separate linearity and low/high diagnostics, the 2015--2017 earlier trend, and exclusion of Malesky's previously criticized paper. Journal/year effects restrict to journals containing both groups: International Organization has only flagged papers. This leaves 19 originals, including the all-zero paper. These are sensitivity analyses of a small diagnostic-specific comparison, not independent studies to pool. \section{Synthesis across methodological audits and replication evidence}\label{app:synthesis}
We weight study log relative-growth contrasts by inverse estimated sampling variance: $w_j=s_j^{-2}/\sum_k s_k^{-2}$ and $\bar\beta=\sum_j w_j\widehat\beta_j$. The reported quantity is $100(e^{\bar\beta}-1)$, with standard error $1/\sqrt{\sum_j s_j^{-2}}$ on the log scale. Normal intervals condition on the included studies and estimated component variances. They do not require identical study effects, but cross-study independence is assumed. Precision weights, timing and the expansion to replication evidence are retrospective choices made after examining individual results.
